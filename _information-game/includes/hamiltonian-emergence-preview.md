\subsection{Saturation and the Correlation Sector}

\notes{When the constraint saturates at $C = C_{\max} = \sum_k \log d_k$, every marginal entropy reaches its maximum $s_k = \log d_k$, forcing every marginal to the maximally mixed state $\rho_k = I/d_k$. All structure then resides entirely in the *joint* correlations [@Lawrence-hamiltonian26].

The admissible perturbations form the *correlation sector*
$$
\mathcal{C} = \{ X : \operatorname{tr}_{\neq k}(X) = 0 \text{ for all } k \},
$$
the common kernel of all single-site partial traces. On this tangent space the linearised reversible dynamics is generated by the *compressed* commutator $L_\kappa = \Pi_\kappa\,\mathrm{ad}_{K_0}\,\Pi_\kappa\big|_{\mathcal{C}}$, where $K_0 = -\log\rho_0$ (trace-gauge modular generator) and $\Pi_\kappa$ is the metric projection onto $\mathcal{C}$.}

\slides{
* Saturation pins every marginal: $\rho_k = I/d_k$
* Correlation sector $\mathcal{C} = \ker(\operatorname{tr}_{\neq k})\ \forall k$
* Reversible dynamics: compressed commutator $L_\kappa$ on $\mathcal{C}$
}

\subsection{The Ritz Criterion: Back-fitting a Hamiltonian}

\notes{Heisenberg's 1925 matrix mechanics can be read as a walk calculus on a transition graph: spectral lines are edges, the Ritz combination rule $\omega_{ik} = \omega_{ij} + \omega_{jk}$ is flatness of a $U(1)$ connection, and a Hamiltonian is back-fitted when this connection is exact (admits a vertex potential). The game uses this as a criterion, not an assumption: a Hamiltonian exists precisely when the oscillating modes of $L_\kappa$ satisfy Ritz closure [@Lawrence-hamiltonian26].}

\slides{
* Ritz combination rule $\omega_{ik} = \omega_{ij} + \omega_{jk}$ 
  * a flatness condition
* Back-fitted Hamiltonian iff connection is *exact* (vertex potential exists)
* This is the criterion, not an assumption
}

\subsection{Compliance Dichotomy and the Dressing Field}

\notes{Saturated modes split into two compliance classes. *Vacuously compliant* modes (those with at least two traceless site factors) already lie in $\mathcal{C}$: the constraint cannot see them and they oscillate at their bare modular gaps. *Actively compliant* modes must be projected, dragging a compensating admixture; they pay a spectral price, i.e.\ a *dressing factor* $\lambda_\kappa < 1$, so that
$$
\omega = x\lambda_\kappa, \qquad \lambda_\kappa = \sqrt{\frac{d^2-4}{d^2-4+4\eta}}, \quad \eta = \frac{\kappa_{QQ}}{\kappa_\times}.
$$
For a qubit pair ($d=2$), $\lambda_\kappa = 0$: no oscillating admissible modes exist. The qutrit pair ($d = 3$) is the minimal bipartite clock [@Lawrence-hamiltonian26].}

\slides{
* Vacuous compliance: bare frequency $x$ (thermmal-time sector)
* Active compliance: dressed frequency $x\lambda_\kappa < x$
* $\lambda_\kappa = \sqrt{(d^2{-}4)/(d^2{-}4{+}4\eta)}$
* Qubit pairs: no clock; qutrit pairs: minimal clock
}

\subsection{Composite Backgrounds, Holonomy, and Locality}

\notes{A composite background (e.g.\ two clusters) populates both compliance classes. Both dressed and bare modes connect the *same* level pair, producing a multigraph with a *digon*. The dressing field is non-constant on this component — taking value $1$ on the bare edge and $\lambda_\kappa$ on the dressed edge — so the $U(1)$ connection has holonomy $e^{ix(1-\lambda_\kappa)t}$. Global Ritz closure fails: the composite walks on a *magnetic* multigraph.

The two rival flat subgraphs are present simultaneously: the *generated* (dressed) subgraph carries the back-fitted cluster Hamiltonian; the *inherited* (bare) subgraph is the thermal-time sector, present but demoted to interaction terms.

Locality emerges from the no-barber principle itself. A global clock across $N$ parties requires a flat frame connection on a cyclic party graph — precisely what no-barber forbids, since only an external frame could enforce cycle-compatibility. Pairwise and cluster backgrounds are forests in the party graph (no cycles, no coordination needed) and are therefore the generic clock structures compatible with the game [@Lawrence-hamiltonian26].}

\slides{
* Composite: two compliance classes on same level pair — a *digon*
* Holonomy: $e^{ix(1-\lambda_\kappa)t}$ — flat connection breaks
* Two rival flat subgraphs: generated (dressed) vs inherited (bare/thermal-time)
* Locality from no-barber: global clock needs external frame, local clocks don't
}

\narration{The third paper is about how Hamiltonian structure emerges. The framing has changed significantly from the version I presented at DAMTP. The key condition is no longer a "Gibbs-locked" alignment — it's saturation. When the constraint saturates, all marginals are pinned to the maximally mixed state and all structure lives in the correlations. The oscillating modes of the compressed commutator on the correlation sector are the candidates for Hamiltonian transitions. Whether a Hamiltonian can be back-fitted is the Ritz criterion: do those modes satisfy the combination rule $\omega_{ik} = \omega_{ij} + \omega_{jk}$?

There's a beautiful compliance dichotomy. Modes with at least two traceless site factors are invisible to every marginal — the constraint cannot see them, so they pass through undressed and oscillate at the bare modular gap. These are the thermal-time modes. Single-site modes must be projected to stay within the correlation sector; they pay a spectral price, the dressing factor $\lambda_\kappa$. For qubits, $\lambda_\kappa = 0$ — no clock. For qutrits, you get the minimal bipartite clock.

Composite backgrounds create a multigraph with parallel edges: a digon connecting the same level pair at two different frequencies — dressed and bare. The connection curves. There are two rival flat subgraphs. And locality emerges from the no-barber principle: a global clock across $N$ parties would require a flat frame connection on a cyclic party graph, which only an external frame could enforce. Pairwise and cluster clocks are forests — no cycles, no coordination, no barber needed.}
