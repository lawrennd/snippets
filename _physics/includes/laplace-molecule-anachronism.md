\subsection{Laplace's Molecule: an Anachronism?}

\slides{Last week Laplace spoke of ``a simple molecule of air.'' That sounds odd once you meet Boltzmann.}

\slidesincremental{
* Laplace, *Essai* (1814): the *curve* of a molecule is as determined as a planet's
* Boltzmann dies in 1906 still fighting atomists' opponents
* Atoms / molecules were *not* settled science in the nineteenth century
}

\speakernotes{Callback to lecture 1 quote. Do not resolve yet --- ask why Laplace can talk this way a century before Perrin. Answer is Bernoulli's kinetic tradition, not modern chemistry.}

\notes{In lecture 1 we read Laplace's line that the curve of a simple molecule of air or vapour is regulated as certainly as a planetary orbit, the difference being only our ignorance [@Laplace-essai14]. To a modern ear that is anachronistic. Ludwig Boltzmann spent his career defending a molecular picture of matter; energeticists such as Ostwald and positivists such as Mach treated atoms as optional or metaphysical. Boltzmann died in 1906; Einstein's Brownian-motion theory and Perrin's experiments (1908--1909) are what made the molecular hypothesis unavoidable for the mainstream. So why is Laplace, writing in 1814, already talking about molecules?}

\newslide{``Molecule'' Before Chemistry Won}

\slides{Laplace is not citing Dalton. He is speaking Newton's corpuscular language.}

\slidesincremental{
* French *molécule*: a small particle of matter, not yet the chemist's molecule
* The kinetic idea is older than the chemical proof of atoms
* The gremlin is ignorance of that particle's *path*, not a claim that atoms were established
}

\notes{In early modern and Enlightenment usage, *molecule* often meant a minute body or corpuscle --- the Newtonian residue of a particulate theory of matter --- rather than the stoichiometrically defined chemical molecule of the late nineteenth century. Laplace's point in the *Essai* is epistemological: even if every air particle followed a determined trajectory, we lack the data, the laws in usable form, and the computation to track it, so we turn to probability. The ontological fight over whether those particles *exist* is a later, sharper controversy. The person who gave Laplace a concrete kinetic picture of air was Daniel Bernoulli, three-quarters of a century earlier.}
