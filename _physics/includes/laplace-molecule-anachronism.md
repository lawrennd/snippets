\subsection{Laplace's Molecule: an Anachronism?}

\slides{Laplace spoke of "a simple molecule of air." Sounds odd once you meet Boltzmann.}

\slidesincremental{
* Laplace, *Essai* (1814): the *curve* of a molecule is as determined as a planet's
* Boltzmann dies in 1906 still fighting atomists' opponents
* Atoms / molecules were *not* settled science in the nineteenth century
}

\speakernotes{Callback to lecture 1 quote. Do not resolve yet, ask why Laplace can talk this way a century before Perrin. Answer is Bernoulli's kinetic tradition, not modern chemistry.}

\notes{Laplace's gremlin suggests that the curve of a simple molecule of air or vapour is regulated as certainly as a planetary orbit, the difference being our ignorance [@Laplace-essai14]. With some thought, this might sound anachronistic. Laplace is writing in 1814, and yet Ludwig Boltzmann spent his career defending atomic perspective on matter. Energeticists such as Ostwald and positivists such as Mach treated atoms as optional or metaphysical. Boltzmann died in 1906 and it was only after Einstein's Brownian-motion theory and Perrin's experiments (1908--1909) that the molecular hypothesis becaome  unavoidable for the mainstream. So why is Laplace, writing in 1814, already talking about molecules?}

\newslide{"Molecule" Before Chemistry Won}

\slides{Laplace is not citing Dalton. He is speaking Newton's corpuscular language.}

\slidesincremental{
* French *molécule*: a small particle of matter, not yet the chemist's molecule
* The kinetic idea is older than the chemical proof of atoms
* The gremlin is ignorance of that particle's *path*, not a claim that atoms were established
}

\notes{In early modern and Enlightenment usage, *molecule* often meant a minute body or corpuscle. It is the Newtonian residue of a particulate theory of matter, not yet a stoichiometrically defined chemical molecule of the late nineteenth century. Laplace's point in the *Essai* is epistemological: even if these air molecules followed a determined trajectory, we lack the data, the laws in usable form, and the computation to track it, so we turn to probability. The ontological fight over whether those particles *exist* is yet to come. But the fact that they seem not to exist (at some scales) and that the argument can even occur is interesting. Why don't we perceive these molecules at particular scales? That is a property of coarse-graining in physics, and (for me) it feels closely related to the way we abstract concepts in our intelligence.}

\notes{Laplace's perspective on air comes from Daniel Bernoulli, who preceded him by three-quarters of a century.}
