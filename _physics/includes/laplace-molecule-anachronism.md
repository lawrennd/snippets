\subsection{Laplace's Molecule: an Anachronism?}

\slides{Laplace spoke of "a simple molecule of air." That sounds anachronistic once we meet Boltzmann.}

\slidesincremental{
* Laplace, *Essai* (1814): the *curve* of a molecule is as determined as a planet's
* Boltzmann dies in 1906 still fighting atomists' opponents
* Atoms / molecules were *not* settled science in the nineteenth century
}

\speakernotes{Callback to lecture 1 quote. Do not resolve yet, ask why Laplace can talk this way a century before Perrin. Answer is Bernoulli's kinetic tradition, not modern chemistry.}

\notes{How does that notion of entropy relate to our notion of information? This is a contested space. I am giving you my perspective; you will be able to explore whether it is contested in other works.

Laplace's gremlin suggests that the curve of a simple molecule of air or vapour is regulated as certainly as a planetary orbit, the difference being our ignorance [@Laplace-essai14]. With some thought, this might sound anachronistic. He is writing in 1814. He predates atomic theory as physicists later understood it, and he predates the chemist's molecule. So what is his notion of a molecule? He is saying that its motion is determined as a planet's is.

Boltzmann, operating nearly a hundred years after Laplace, is trying to produce a molecular or atomic theory of matter, and that claim is disputed in his time. Other physicists disagree with him. In chemistry the story is quite different, because they have the periodic table. In physics the notion of a molecule is not there. Boltzmann takes his own life in 1906, and one of the reasons is thought to be his struggle to have the atomic theory accepted.

Einstein's *annus mirabilis* is 1905. Special relativity and the photoelectric effect dominate the retelling, but one of the papers is on Brownian motion. He does not discover Brownian motion --- that is Robert Brown, about fifty years earlier --- but he writes stochastic differential equations to describe it. That is a molecular theory, in effect, and it is not validated by experiment for a couple of years. Boltzmann dies without his ideas being accepted. Energeticists such as Ostwald and positivists such as Mach treated atoms as optional or metaphysical. Only after Perrin's experiments (1908--1909) did the molecular hypothesis become unavoidable for the mainstream. So why is Laplace, writing in 1814, already talking about molecules?}

\newslide{"Molecule" Before Chemistry Won}

\slides{Laplace is not citing Dalton. He is speaking Newton's corpuscular language.}

\slidesincremental{
* French *molécule*: a small particle of matter, not yet the chemist's molecule
* The kinetic idea is older than the chemical proof of atoms
* The gremlin is ignorance of that particle's *path*, not a claim that atoms were established
}

\notes{In early modern and Enlightenment usage, *molecule* often meant a minute body or corpuscle. It is the Newtonian residue of a particulate theory of matter, not yet a stoichiometrically defined chemical molecule of the late nineteenth century. Laplace is not citing Dalton, who comes from the chemical perspective. He is citing Newton, who has a corpuscular language.

The English translation we have says molecule. Perhaps it should be corpuscle. I do not know enough about the language and the history; that is an open question. The word is coming to us as a translation of French, and my supposition is that it enters English as a scientific word only once we have a chemical sense of it.

When we read old science we tend to project our modern context onto it, particularly if a translator has rendered *molécule* as molecule, where the contemporary English might have been corpuscle.

Laplace's point in the *Essai* is epistemological: even if these air molecules followed a determined trajectory, we lack the data, the laws in usable form, and the computation to track it, so we turn to probability. The gremlin is still valid, because Laplace's idea does carry through. That is our modern notion of how air is made up. The gremlin is ignorance of the particle's path. It is not a claim that atoms were established. The ontological fight over whether those particles *exist* is yet to come. Why do we not perceive these molecules at particular scales? That is a property of coarse-graining in physics, and for me it is closely related to the way we abstract concepts in our intelligence.}

\notes{Laplace's perspective on air comes from Daniel Bernoulli, who preceded him by three-quarters of a century.}
