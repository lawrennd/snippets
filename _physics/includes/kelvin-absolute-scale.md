\subsection{Thomson's Scale from the Mill}

\notes{William Thomson, who later became Lord Kelvin, takes Carnot seriously as theory. In 1848 he is a twenty-four year old professor of natural philosophy at Glasgow, and he has read Clapeyron's recasting of the *Réflexions*. Fahrenheit, Celsius and the air thermometer already exist: ice and steam already mark $0$ and $100$ on the scale he will compare against. Those pins are a century old. What does not exist is a meaning of *degree* that is independent of mercury, or of air. If the motive power of heat depends only on the quantity of caloric and the height of its fall, then that height should be usable as a thermometer [@Thomson-absolute48]. That is what he adds at twenty-four: a definition of the interval, not a new pair of fixed points, and not a bottom of the fall. The 1848 answer still assumes that heat is conserved. It is not yet the scale that later bears his name.}

\newslide{William Thomson}

\figure{\includejpg{\diagramsDir/physics/william-thomson-kelvin}{38%}}{William Thomson (1824--1907), later Lord Kelvin. Photograph from *The World's Work*, 1902 (Wikimedia Commons, public domain) --- half a century after the 1848 scale.}{william-thomson-kelvin}

\notes{Thomson studied as an undergraduate at Cambridge and spent his career as professor of natural philosophy at Glasgow. Maxwell is a younger contemporary in the same group: people who combined the empirical work going on in places like Glasgow, where they really cared about the engineering, with a deep mathematical understanding. In 1848 Thomson is looking for a thermometric scale that is not an accident of mercury or air. He believes that Carnot's theory provides such a scale. He already has a quantity of heat: Black's unit, the heat that raises a kilogramme of water by one degree of the air thermometer. What he still lacks is a meaning for an *interval* of temperature that does not belong to one substance. When he is twenty-four he tries to interpret the degree as a measurement related to specific heat: how much work could you extract from one unit of Black's heat falling one degree. Carnot's mill supplies it: the motive power of a given quantity of caloric falling through one degree. That is mill-work from a *fall*, as water on a wheel.}

\speakernotes{Call him Thomson. Glasgow, 1848, age 24. Do not call this the Kelvin scale. Do not mention Joule. Fahrenheit already exists. He is not moving ice and steam. He is saying what a degree is.}

\newslide{What a Degree Is}

\slides{The pins are a century old. He is defining the *degree*.}

\slidesincremental{
* Fahrenheit, Celsius, air: a degree is equal *expansion* of one substance
* Thomson, aged 24: a degree is an equal *fall* of caloric --- the same motive power from the same quantity of heat, anywhere on the scale
* Ice remains $0$, steam remains $100$. He has not moved the pins. He has defined the rungs.
}

\notes{The air thermometer is the scale Thomson is arguing with. Household thermometers use mercury because it is convenient; in accurate work you compare the mercury against air and quote the air reading (Regnault). Air is a better expansion-substance than mercury, but it is still a substance. A degree that means ``equal expansion of air'' is not independent of the thermometer. Thomson will not call that absolute.

What he adds is a meaning for the interval. Take a unit of heat --- Black already told you how to measure that --- and let it fall through Carnot's mill. Call one degree whatever drop of temperature always yields the same motive power from that unit, whether you are near steam or near ice. The degree now belongs to the mill, not to mercury, and not to air. Independence of substance here means independence of the thermometer's fluid and of the engine's working fluid. The fixed points stay where Fahrenheit and Celsius left them. That is why the paper matters in 1848, before anyone has broken conservation of heat.}

\speakernotes{Why bother, if ice and steam are already marked? Because a mercury degree and an air degree are not the same mill-height. He is making the rungs equal. He is twenty-four. Caloric is still conserved.}

\newslide{An Absolute Thermometer}

\slides{A degree is a height of fall of conserved heat. Ice is still $0$, steam still $100$.}

\slidesincremental{
* Take a unit of heat from a body at $T$ on this scale
* Let it fall to a body at $T-1$; the same unit arrives
* A perfect mill in between always gives the same motive power, whatever $T$ is
}

\notes{Thomson's own statement of the characteristic property: all degrees have the same value; a unit of heat descending from a body $A$ at temperature $T^\circ$ to a body $B$ at $(T-1)^\circ$ gives out the same mechanical effect, whatever the number $T$. That is the mill, turned into a ruler. The working fluid is still caloric. The fall is still a fall, not a conversion. He then *compares* the new ruler with the air thermometer, using Clapeyron's formulae and Regnault's steam data. The two scales are forced to agree at ice and at steam. Everything else is a table of how unequal an air-thermometer degree is, in mill-value, as you go up the scale. Work is how we make the degrees equal. It is not how we make the heat, and it is not a new zero.}

\newslide{Is There a Bottom?}

\slides{In 1848 the mill has no bottom. Infinite cold is infinitely far down.}

\slidesincremental{
* Air already suggests a wall: its volume would vanish at about $-273^\circ$
* That number is Gay-Lussac's expansion, not Carnot's mill
* Thomson still pins $0$ at ice; on his mill scale you can always fall one more equal degree
}

\notes{The $-273^\circ$ that looks like absolute zero is already sitting in the air thermometer --- the same bulb of air, volume as the reading. If that volume is linear in temperature, pushing the graduation far enough reduces the air to nothing at $-100/0.366$, which Thomson writes as $-273^\circ$. He uses that fact only as a reason why Clapeyron's table was to be expected: an air-thermometer degree is *worth more* when it is cold, because a finite number of those degrees is trying to reach infinite cold. It is not the zero of his scale, and he does not compute a table down to it. On the mill scale a unit of heat can always be let down one more equal-value degree, and still arrive. Infinite cold is at $-\infty$. 1848 has done something narrower: it has made a degree a Carnot object. There is not yet a bottom, because caloric is still conserved.}

\speakernotes{Do not sell 1848 as discovering $0\,\mathrm{K}$. The pins are already known. The air thermometer's $-273$ is vanishing volume. The mill still has no floor.}

\newslide{Heat Is Not Destroyed}

\slides{He still thinks with Carnot. Heat is conserved. The mill turns because caloric *falls*.}

\slidesincremental{
* A unit of heat that leaves the hot body arrives at the cold
* Motive power is the mill, not heat being used up
* ``The conversion of heat into mechanical effect is probably impossible, certainly undiscovered''
}

\notes{Thomson writes that in the present state of science no operation is known by which heat can be absorbed without either raising a temperature or becoming latent. Conversion of heat, or caloric, into mechanical effect is probably impossible, certainly undiscovered. Actual engines must therefore look for their power not in absorption and conversion, but in the transmission of heat. That is Carnot, stated as a fact of 1848, and it is the assumption his mill-degree rests on.}

\speakernotes{Read the quote. He is twenty-four. Caloric still falls. Do not bring in Joule. Next is the paddle wheel.}
