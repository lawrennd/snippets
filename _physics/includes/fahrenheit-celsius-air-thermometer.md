\subsection{Mercury, Ice, Steam, and Air}

\notes{Black already treats temperature as a *reading* and heat as a *quantity*. The reading, in the 1840s, comes from one of three expansion thermometers. Fahrenheit and Celsius use mercury. Accurate laboratory work uses air. All three pin two fixed points --- ice and steam, or a close cousin --- and then divide the interval so that equal expansions of the chosen substance are equal degrees. Thomson will inherit that practice, and then refuse it as a definition of a degree.}

\newslide{Fahrenheit and Celsius}

\figure{\includejpg{\diagramsDir/physics/fahrenheit-thermometer}{42%}}{Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit (1686--1736) with a mercury thermometer. Anonymous engraving, 18th century (Wikimedia Commons, public domain). The instrument is mercury in glass; the scale is equal expansion of that mercury between two pins.}{fahrenheit-thermometer}

\slides{A degree is an equal expansion of mercury. Ice and steam are the pins.}

\slidesincremental{
* Fahrenheit (1724): mercury; ice at $32$, steam at $212$
* Celsius (1742): the same two pins, later numbered $0$ and $100$
* Neither scale is heat. Both are the expansion of one liquid
}

\notes{Fahrenheit's mercury thermometer is the household instrument of the eighteenth century. He marks a mixture of ice, water and sal-ammoniac as $0$, and (originally) blood heat as $96$. The scale that survives is a recalibration of the same tube: ice at $32$, steam at $212$, so there are $180$ equal expansions of mercury between the two pins. Celsius, in 1742, uses the same pins on a centesimal division. His own numbering ran the other way --- $100$ at ice, $0$ at steam --- and was inverted within a few years. By Thomson's time ``Celsius'' means ice $0$, steam $100$. The physics is the same as Fahrenheit: you have chosen a substance, and you have called equal expansions of it equal degrees. Black's whole point is that this reading is *not* the quantity of heat. The same rise of the same mercury takes different heat in water, in oil, in ice that is melting.}

\speakernotes{Hold up the mercury tube. Two pins, ice and steam. Fahrenheit or Celsius is only how you number the interval. Do not call this heat. Black already split those. Thomson is about to ask what a *degree* should be if it is not this expansion.}

\newslide{The Air Thermometer}

\figure{\includepng{\diagramsDir/physics/air-thermometer-scheme}{72%}}{(a) Constant *pressure*: a sliding mercury index, open to the room; the volume of the trapped air is the reading. (b) Constant *volume*: a mercury manometer; the height $h$ is the reading. Scheme by P.\ Peterlin (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0). Thomson's 1848 comparison is with (a).}{air-thermometer-scheme}

\slides{The laboratory standard is air, not mercury. Pressure is held at the room's.}

\slidesincremental{
* Trap a mass of air behind mercury that is open to the atmosphere
* Slide the thread, or lower the cistern, until the mercury surfaces are level
* Volume is then the reading; ice and steam still pin $0$ and $100$
}

\notes{The air thermometer is the 1840s laboratory standard, the scale to which mercury and alcohol are reduced (Regnault). Take a glass bulb of dry air and confine it with mercury. The mercury is open to the room: either a sliding thread in a capillary, as in (a), or a cistern you raise and lower. You move the mercury until the two surfaces sit at the same height, so the trapped air is at atmospheric pressure. The *volume* of that air is then the reading. Ice water and steam still fix two points; the interval is divided so that equal increases of volume are equal degrees. That is Gay-Lussac made into an instrument: far from condensation, the volume of air rises almost linearly with this temperature. Push the same line far enough and the volume would vanish at about $-273^\circ$ of the air scale. That number lives in the instrument, not in Carnot. Panel (b) is the other common form, a constant-*volume* gas thermometer, where you read pressure from the manometer height. Thomson in 1848 is arguing with (a). Air is a better expansion-substance than mercury --- different gases agree closely unless they are about to liquefy --- but it is still a substance. A degree that means ``equal expansion of air'' is not yet independent of the thermometer.}

\speakernotes{This is the apparatus. Mercury open to the room, surfaces level, pressure of the trapped air equals the barometer. Volume is the degree. Ice $0$, steam $100$, same pins as Celsius. Still air. Kelvin's question is: what is a degree if it is not this?}
