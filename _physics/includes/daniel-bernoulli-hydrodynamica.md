\subsection{Hydrodynamica}

\slides{Daniel Bernoulli (1738) already treats a gas as many tiny bodies in motion.}

\speakernotes{Wing cartoon: particle lines around an aerofoil that "must" go faster because the path is longer --- why do they not just arrive late? You are mixing grains. Keep Johann appropriating the work in notes, not on the slide.}

\notes{When Laplace spoke of the curve of a simple molecule of air, he may well have been thinking of work from about seventy-five years earlier, bridging Newton to Laplace: Daniel Bernoulli (1700--1782), and *Hydrodynamica*.

People know the Bernoulli effect: constrict a flow, the fluid speeds up, the pressure changes --- the cartoon of what lets planes fly. The diagrams draw particle lines around a wing and say that this particle goes around the top, that one around the bottom, and the upper one has to go faster because the path is longer. Why does it not simply arrive late? Fluid dynamics is hard to have a good sense of because we are working across different coarse grains.

Daniel Bernoulli, I think, is the first person to take a corpuscular model of a fluid and treat the particles as sitting in a little element, as we do in a modern discretisation. He effectively uses conservation of energy, which is striking, because Newton had treated momentum. I think it is Huygens who does conservation of energy.

He was the son of Johann Bernoulli --- uncle Jacob was the other giant of that generation; Johann's other student was Euler. Johann treated his son with remarkable hostility: he took the work, published it under his own name, and backdated it. That is why Daniel ended up in St Petersburg, at a time when Russia was founding universities, and Euler joined him there for a while. He produced a large body of work; this one was read an enormous amount. He is writing in Latin, in 1738, already treating a gas as many tiny bodies in motion.}
