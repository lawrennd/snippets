\subsection{Thomson Returns: The Scale After Joule}

\notes{Clausius has kept Carnot's limit and dropped conserved heat. Thomson does the same, independently, a year later. In 1851 he writes the dynamical theory of heat [@Thomson-dynamical51]. The mill is still there. The ruler is not. Heat taken in and heat rejected now stand in a *ratio* of the two temperatures, and for the first time the fall has a bottom.}

\newslide{Thomson, 1851}

\slides{The 1848 scale assumed conserved heat. After Joule that assumption is gone.}

\slidesincremental{
* Clausius, 1850: two laws; Carnot's ceiling without caloric conservation
* Thomson, 1851: accepts Joule; heat is not a conserved fluid
* What survives: a reversible engine still has a limit that depends only on the two temperatures
}

\notes{The 1848 paper had already heard of Joule, in a footnote, and had refused him: conversion of heat into work was probably impossible, certainly undiscovered. The paddle wheel is the experiment Thomson said had not been shown. By 1851 he accepts it. Heat can be generated by work, and some of the heat taken from the hot bath does not arrive at the cold bath --- it has become work. Conservation of caloric cannot be the general law. Clausius had already said so. Thomson's contribution, for us, is what happens to the *scale*.}

\speakernotes{Call him Thomson still. 1851, not 1848. Clausius has already written the two laws. Thomson is rewriting the thermometer, not discovering the second law.}

\newslide{The Ratio, Not the Fall}

\slides{Equal mill-degrees are replaced by a proportion of the two heats.}

\slidesincremental{
* Reversible engine: $\displaystyle\frac{Q_h}{T_h} = \frac{Q_c}{T_c}$
* The same identity Clausius wrote as $\mathrm{d}S = \mathrm{d}Q_{\mathrm{rev}}/T$
* Absolute temperatures are now those for which this ratio holds
}

\notes{In 1848 a degree was a height of fall of a conserved unit of heat: the same mill-work for every rung, whatever $T$ you started from. After Joule the unit does not arrive. What *is* equal, for a reversible engine, is $Q_h/T_h$ and $Q_c/T_c$. That is Clausius's entropy leaving the hot bath and arriving at the cold bath. Thomson takes it as the definition of the scale. Ice is no longer the zero. The numbers $T_h$ and $T_c$ are the absolute temperatures of the two baths; their ratio is the ratio of the heat taken in to the heat rejected.}

\newslide{Now There Is a Bottom}

\slides{Absolute zero is where a reversible engine would reject no heat.}

\slidesincremental{
* $T=0$ is $Q_c=0$ for a perfect mill between $T_h$ and the floor
* The air thermometer's $-273^\circ$ was vanishing volume; this is vanishing rejected heat
* Infinite cold is no longer infinitely far down the mill
}

\notes{This is the genuine floor that 1848 did not have. If $Q_c/Q_h = T_c/T_h$, then $T_c=0$ is the temperature at which a reversible engine, taking $Q_h$ from the hot bath, rejects nothing. The $-273^\circ$ of Gay-Lussac's expansion was an accident of air. The zero of the 1851 scale is an accident of *engines*: it is the temperature you cannot fall below, because there is no heat left to reject. We still use this scale. We name it after the title Thomson later took.}

\speakernotes{1848: ice is zero, infinite cold at $-\infty$. 1851: $T=0$ is $Q_c=0$. Do not linger on the later title Lord Kelvin --- the paper is Thomson's.}
