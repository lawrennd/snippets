\subsection{Count Rumford's Cannon}

\notes{Carnot treated heat as a conserved fluid because that is what a mill requires. The difficulty with that picture was already in print. In 1798, twenty-six years before the *Réflexions*, Benjamin Thompson, who styled himself Count Rumford, published a paper in the *Philosophical Transactions* on heat excited by friction [@Rumford-friction98].}

\newslide{Count Rumford}

\figure{\includejpg{\diagramsDir/physics/count-rumford}{40%}}{Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford (1753--1814). American-born, then in the service of the Elector of Bavaria. Portrait by Moritz Kellerhoven (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).}{count-rumford}

\notes{Rumford was the superintendent for the boring of brass cannons in the military arsenal at Munich. He is struck by how hot the gun becomes, and by how much hotter the metallic chips are (he checks and they are hotter than boiling water). His paper to the royal society asks where does the heat come from?}

\speakernotes{Flashback, not a new founding myth. Dates: 1798 paper, Munich arsenal. Do not say this killed caloric. Carnot still uses caloric in 1824.}

\newslide{The Cannon}

\slides{A blunt borer, a brass cylinder, a box of water, horses turning the gun.}

\slidesincremental{
* About $18.8\,\mathrm{lb}$ of water, starting at $60^\circ\mathrm{F}$
* The cylinder at about $32$ turns a minute
* After two hours and thirty minutes the water boils --- with no fire
}

\notes{He asks whether the chips have changed their capacity for heat, as Black's latent-heat doctrine would require if caloric were being squeezed out of the metal, but he finds they haven't. He builds an experiment with a short hollow cylinder is turned against a blunt steel borer by machinery carried round by horses. A wooden box filled with water surrounds the cylinder. At one hour the water is at $107^\circ\mathrm{F}$; at two hours $178^\circ\mathrm{F}$; at two hours thirty minutes it boils.}

\newslide{Rumford's Apparatus}

\figure{\includejpg{\diagramsDir/physics/rumford-cannon-boring}{55%}}{Rumford's 1798 apparatus, as reprinted in *The Complete Works of Count Rumford* (1870). Fig.~2: the cannon in the boring machine, turned by the bar $w$. Fig.~3: the same cylinder in the water box that is brought to the boil.}{rumford-cannon-boring}

\speakernotes{Fig.~3 is the mechanism: blunt borer inside, water outside. The heat is not coming from the chips, and it does not run out.}

\newslide{Inexhaustible}

\slides{Furnishing heat without limitation ...}

\slidesincremental{
* The chips do not lose a hidden store of caloric
* The heat continues as long as the work continues
* Rumford's conclusion: heat is a *mode of motion*
}

\notes{Rumford remarks that the the source of the heat generated by friction appeared evidently to be *inexhaustible*. But anything which an insulated body can continue to furnish without limitation cannot possibly be a material substance. That is 1798. It is a qualitative blow to caloric, not a mechanical equivalent. He does not weigh a known quantity of work against a known quantity of heat. He has shown generation. He has shown that the thermodynamic ledger doesn't close.}

\speakernotes{Quote: inexhaustible, therefore not a substance. Motion, not a number. Joule will supply the number. Carnot never had to face this, because his question was a cycle, not friction.}
